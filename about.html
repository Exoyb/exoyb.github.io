The bits people usually ask me

Pick whatever matters to you. You don’t need to read my life story from top to bottom.

Why Cybersecurity?It started with gaming, DDoS attacks and refusing to accept “the internet broke” as an answer.

My interest started through online gaming. After being knocked offline by DDoS attacks, I wanted to understand what had actually happened: what the attack was doing, why it worked and what could be done to stop it.

That curiosity spread into networking, malware, spoofing, software behaviour and attack techniques. I kept coming back to the same questions: What happened? How? Why? What evidence proves it?

Why now?This is a deliberate move built on years of troubleshooting, not a sudden career reset.

I’ve spent years building the pieces without originally calling them “security skills”: diagnosing hardware, testing electronics, dealing with fraud risk, supporting software, handling permissions and authentication issues, documenting fixes and escalating complex problems.

Now I’m deliberately turning that experience toward security. I’m studying the fundamentals, building hands-on labs and documenting the journey so employers can see the progression rather than just take my word for it.

Why Security Operations / SOC?Because investigation, triage and evidence are the parts of technical work I naturally gravitate toward.

SOC work appeals to me because it turns curiosity into a process: something happened, there is evidence somewhere, and the job is to work out what matters, what caused it and what should happen next.

That maps well to how I already approach support. I like narrowing noisy problems into something specific, reproducing behaviour where possible, separating symptoms from causes and handing over useful context when escalation is needed.

Signal → Evidence → Triage → Root cause → Action → Documentation

What do I bring from support?Technical diagnosis matters, but so do communication, ownership and knowing when to escalate.

Support has taught me to work with incomplete information, ask better questions, stay calm when someone is frustrated and keep ownership of a problem even when the eventual fix sits with another team.

At Caseware I also write technical knowledge-base content, so I’m used to turning a one-off solution into guidance someone else can follow. For an entry-level SOC role, I see that as just as important as knowing a tool: good analysts leave useful notes.
