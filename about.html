Why Cybersecurity?Gaming, DDoS attacks and refusing to accept “the internet broke” as an answer.

My interest in Cybersecurity started from two main places.

I’m very much a night owl, and when I was younger, in my early to mid teens, my internet would be cut off at 12:00am through the router settings. I couldn’t guess the router’s Wi-Fi password because it had been changed from the default, so what could I do?

I started trying to understand how the restriction actually worked. What specifically was being blocked? How did the router know which device was mine? Why did the restriction persist after a reboot?

That unlocked the home cyber pirate in me.

I started learning what DNS was, what subnets were, how IP addresses worked, the difference between IPv4 and IPv6, and most importantly, what a MAC address was. Eventually I worked out that I could disconnect from the network, change my PC name, spoof my MAC address, renew my IP configuration and reconnect as what appeared to be a different device.

I was back online again 🙌

My parents are actually very tech savvy themselves. They checked the network logs, knew exactly what I was doing and, much to my surprise, encouraged me to keep learning rather than simply shutting it down.

The second thing happened around the same time. I played a lot of online games, and occasionally after beating someone they would threaten to DDoS me. Naturally, I thought, “nahhh, no way, all bark and no bite.”

Five minutes later, I was offline.

That sent me down another rabbit hole. What is a DDoS attack? How does it work? What is the difference between DoS and DDoS? What does a ping actually do? Why can an attack knock somebody offline? How can you protect yourself from it? What is a VPN, and how do you configure one?

And, being a teenager, there was obviously another question: could I send one back?

For legal and professional purposes, of course I would never suggest that I retaliated. How childish would that be?

Those two experiences opened up a whole world of fascination with computers, networks and Cybersecurity. I realised I enjoyed understanding what was happening underneath the surface, figuring out why something worked, and learning how systems could be manipulated, protected and improved.

Why now?A deliberate move built on years of troubleshooting, not a sudden career reset.

I’ve spent years building the pieces without originally calling them security skills: diagnosing hardware, testing electronics, supporting software, handling permissions and authentication issues, documenting fixes and escalating complex problems.

Now I’m deliberately running that experience and natural interest toward security. I have managed to get into the IT industry with Caseware after many years in retail customer service roles. Now I want to progress into Cybersecurity.

Why Security Operations / SOC?Investigation, triage and evidence are the parts of technical work I naturally gravitate toward.

SOC work appeals to me because it turns curiosity into a process: something happened, evidence exists somewhere, and the job is to work out what matters, what caused it and what should happen next.

That maps well to how I already approach support: narrow noisy problems, reproduce behaviour where possible, separate symptoms from causes and hand over useful context when escalation is needed.

What got me into tech?Before SaaS support, I spent years repairing devices, testing electronics and managing technical teams.

What do I bring from support?Technical diagnosis matters, but so do communication, ownership and knowing when to escalate.

Support has taught me how to deal with a constant flow of issues while still keeping the quality of the response high. That means prioritising tickets, understanding what actually needs attention first, communicating clearly with customers and keeping calm when things get busy.

A big part of the job is also making sure the technical side is documented properly. I’m used to writing clear resolution details, recording what has already been tried, explaining the outcome and leaving enough context for someone else to pick the case up without having to start again.

I think that transfers well into Security Operations. Good technical work is not just finding the issue. It is being able to explain what happened, what you checked, what matters, what still needs investigating and when something needs to be escalated.
